The problem

Generic gamification rewards
the comfort zone.

Practice drifts to strengths

Everyone gets the same points, badges, and leaderboard — for whatever they already do well. Weak areas stay weak.

The pull decays

Fixed, predictable rewards lose their motivational force with exposure — and, the evidence suggests, with age.

Across ~20 course sessions, engagement — not content — is the bottleneck.

Who

One user. One gatekeeper.

"Aisha", 24 User

A PGDM student practicing in short bursts between classes. Wants to feel she's improving on weak topics — without busywork or feeling surveilled.

Prof. Singh Buyer · Gatekeeper

Wants classroom engagement and a research artifact. Will not let any AI proposal reach a student unreviewed. He is the yes/no — not the end-user.

Design for the student; sell to the teacher; keep the human in the loop.

How it works

The AI designs the game.
The teacher approves it.

  1. Diagnose. A short baseline (same for all) measures each student's weak spots.
  2. Profile. Practice is personalized to those weak topics.
  3. Design. AI proposes quests + weak-area-weighted rewards — with reasoning the teacher sees.
  4. Approve. Teacher edits / accepts / rejects. Nothing ships unreviewed.
  5. Play & log. The reward resolves; voluntary persistence is captured.

First step

One classroom. Two weeks.
Two tests.

  • The wedge. One gatekeeper (Prof. Singh) + a captive cohort = course-embedded adoption at zero acquisition cost.
  • Test 1 — the reward thesis. A within-subject dry run (8–15 people, condition hidden): does the variable schedule lift voluntary persistence vs. fixed — points held equal?
  • Test 2 — HITL at scale. Timed review: can the teacher clear 20 AI proposals at ≤30–45s each? If not, the core differentiator needs guardrails.

Prove the two riskiest assumptions before building the full economy.
